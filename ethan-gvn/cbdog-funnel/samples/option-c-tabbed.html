1 Hero + Opening Violation

Live Preview Open in new tab →
Belief Shift: "Exercise helps my dog's anxiety" → "Wait... could I be making this worse?"
Key Technique

The Opening Violation - Challenge the core belief instead of validating it.

What This Does

Immediately violates what the reader believes to be true. They arrived thinking exercise helps - you tell them it makes things worse. Creates cognitive tension that demands resolution.

Connection to Funnel Arc

This section creates the "crack" in their belief system. Every section after this widens that crack until the old belief collapses.

Why This Works

Cognitive dissonance. When you contradict a deeply held belief with confidence, the brain can't ignore it. The reader must either dismiss you or keep reading to resolve the tension.

The specificity ("every extra walk, every game of fetch") makes it personal and impossible to dismiss as generic advice. You're describing their behavior, not abstract concepts.

The line "That's not an insult. That's the science." preempts the defensive response that naturally arises when someone feels attacked.

How to Adapt This

Identify the "common wisdom" in your niche that's either wrong or incomplete. Lead with the contradiction.

Formula

"[Thing everyone believes] is [strong negative framing: lie, myth, making it worse]."

Critical Point

The key is confidence - if you hedge, the violation doesn't land. Don't say "might not work." Say "is making it worse."

Lines Worth Studying

  • "That's not an insult. That's the science." - Preempts defensiveness before it forms.
  • "You didn't fail your dog. You followed advice that was never designed for what's actually wrong." - Guilt removal. Shifts blame from reader to the advice.
  • "You can't outrun a chemical imbalance." - Simple, memorable, quotable. Perfect for a belief shift.

2 Reason 1 - The Science Origin Story

Live Preview Open in new tab →
Belief Shift: "The advice must be right" → "Oh, the advice is outdated."
Key Technique

The Origin Story Reframe - Explain where bad advice came from.

What This Does

Explains why the old advice exists. Instead of just saying "it's wrong," you show when it was created and what it was designed for.

People don't like feeling dumb for following advice. By explaining "here's why smart people believed this," you give them an off-ramp. They weren't stupid - they were working with outdated information.

Find the origin of your market's "common knowledge." When was it created? What was it designed for? Formula: "This advice comes from [era]. Back then, we didn't know [new information]."

Lines Worth Studying

  • "The first studies on canine cortisol wouldn't come for another two decades." - Specific detail creates credibility.
