Why you have three versions of this funnel

What's actually different between v5, v6, and v7, and the thinking behind each one.

Side by side, the three page 1s can look like three drafts of the same article. They're not. Each one is a different route to the same destination, and I want you to see the thinking so you can judge them as strategies instead of comparing sentences.

What's identical on purpose

All three open with the same headline: your mom's $56,576. All three end in the same place: the steroids that worked but cap out at thirty days, the months of healing still needed, and the gap that leaves. And all three carry the same idea underneath, that it was never really about Tourette's, it was an infection and the inflammation it left behind.

Page 2 is the same on every version, nearly line for line.

So the only real variable is the route page 1 takes to get a parent to that ending. That's deliberate. When the versions run against each other, the winner tells us something true about the parent, not about lucky wording.

v5 argues the diagnosis was wrong

This is the tightest version, around 2,000 words with seven subheads. Every subhead is a direct claim about the reader's child, and your story sits underneath as the evidence for each claim.

It was never Tourette's. It was an infection nobody closed out.

The bet: a parent who only skims the subheads still receives the entire argument. This is the version for the parent who wants the point made straight, without a long story first.

v6 shows the ending the parent is buying

This is the only version that shows what life looks like on the other side. It walks through an ordinary bedtime, and the mothers who come up to your parents asking what finally worked.

That ordinary evening is the future my parents were trying to save.

The bet: some parents are past being argued with. They've heard confident explanations before and spent money on them. What they haven't seen is proof that the ending exists. v6 sells hope by showing the outcome instead of asserting the logic.

v7 hands the parent their own receipts

This version is built on the record itself: the blood-pressure pill on line one, the hour of calm that wore off, the approved drug that did nothing. Then it turns the list around and asks the parent to run their own.

Nothing failed you. Everything was aimed at the wrong half of the problem.

The bet: the parent most likely to buy is carrying guilt about everything they've already tried. v7 uses their own history as the proof, so the argument convinces and forgives at the same time.

The same $56,576, working three different jobs

v5 explains why the label behind the $56K was wrong. v6 shows what the $56K was trying to buy. v7 makes the $56K list itself the argument.

Why I'm not just picking my favorite

Because I don't get to decide which route converts, parents do. A parent arriving cold from an ad might want the argument, might need to see the ending, or might need to hear it wasn't their fault first. I have a guess about which wins, but I'd rather be told by real clicks than trust my taste.

Run them against each other and the losing versions still earn their keep, because whichever one wins tells us which door these parents walk through, and that shapes every ad we write after.
