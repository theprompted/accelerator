I Took 40 Medications for My Motor Tics. Here's What Finally Stopped Them.

I took forty medications for my motor tics. Not one of them was aimed at what was actually wrong.

I know that now. It took my family eleven years and nineteen doctors to find out why.

On this page I want to show you three things we learned that I wish someone had told my mother on day one.

Why every doctor wrote the same kind of prescription.

What was actually causing the tics that none of them tested for.

And the thing that finally worked after everything else failed.

The reason it worked only makes sense if you understand why everything else did not.

Why every doctor wrote the same prescription (and why it never worked)

The first doctor gave me ten minutes.

He watched me tic, wrote "anxiety" on the chart, and handed my mother a prescription for clonidine.

It is a blood pressure medication. My blood pressure was fine.

Sixty days later we came back. Nothing had changed.

So he raised the dose.

That is how it works.

You walk in, you sit down, you walk out with a new prescription or a higher dose of the old one.

The next doctor writes a different word on the chart. Anxiety becomes Tourette's. Tourette's becomes behavioral.

Each label comes with its own set of prescriptions, and each prescription comes with the same instruction: if it is not working, give him more.

My mother took notes. She kept a notebook across eleven years and nineteen doctors, and if you read it front to back, you can watch the pattern repeat.

Fluoxetine. Started at ten milligrams. Twenty by March. Thirty by May.

Sertraline. Twenty-five milligrams. Doubled within a year.

Abilify. Five. Then ten. Then twenty. Then stopped, because my mother wrote what no doctor had: "There are no improvements with increased dosage."

Forty prescriptions, and the only person who called a stop was the one paying the bills and losing sleep.

Every one of those prescriptions was aimed at the smoke.

The movements. The outbursts. The behavior a teacher could point to.

Nobody in those ten-minute visits had time to ask what was causing the fire.

That is not a conspiracy. That is a waiting room.

What a tic actually feels like from inside (this is what 10-minute visits were prescribing for)

I want to tell you what a tic actually feels like, because the doctors who wrote those prescriptions never asked.

From the outside, it looks like a movement he could stop if he tried.

From the inside, it is heat that works its way up your spine, into the back of your neck, and builds until it has to go somewhere.

The pressure does not let up until the movement happens. Then it starts building again.

It is not a thought. It is not a decision. It is physical pressure with one exit.

My dad described it once as ankle weights nobody else could see.

The boy underneath was fine. But he was carrying something on top of himself that no one could take off for him.

And the ten-minute visits were prescribing for a boy they thought was choosing to keep the weights on.

I was thirteen, standing at the bathroom counter, trying to put down my toothbrush.

It did not feel right.

So I picked it up and put it down again.

Still wrong. Again. Then harder.

Soon I was banging it against the counter, with toothpaste and spit hitting the mirror.

By the time my dad walked in, it looked like I was throwing a fit over a toothbrush.

But inside, I was asking the same question he was:

Why can't you just put it down?

That is what the ten-minute visits were prescribing for.

A pressure they never felt, in a body they spent ten minutes with, using medications designed for conditions I did not have.

The 18 infections his mom tracked that no doctor ever connected to his tics

My mother's notebook has two kinds of entries.

On one side, the medications. Forty prescriptions across eleven years, every one aimed at the tics.

On the other side, buried as a separate problem, eighteen courses of antibiotics.

She had tracked every infection I ever had. She kept the dates, the prescriptions, the doctors.

In 2007, she asked for a strep test herself. It came back positive.

In her own handwriting, next to that entry, she wrote: "Our physician was unknowledgeable about PANDAS."

She was already running the theory. The prescriptions were chasing the label. The infections sat there, in the same notebook, untreated for what they were.

And she spent $13,138 of her own money on an adenoidectomy, immunotherapy, and a tonsillectomy, because she believed the inflammation was connected and no one would listen.

The doctors treating the tics never looked at the infections.

My mother was the only one reading both pages.

One notebook. Two sets of entries.

The fire was in the immune system, and she was the only person who saw it.

The question every mother asks herself at 2am (and why the answer is not what you think)

I know what my mother asked herself at that kitchen table at two in the morning, because I have asked her.

It was not what is the treatment. It was: what did I do wrong? Did I mess my kid up?

She carried that question for eleven years, and no doctor in forty ever took it off her.

Right behind it came the other one: something is not right. You look at your child and you know with total certainty this is not who he is.

He is a different person.

And nobody can tell you what happened.

My mother went looking for the answer in herself. What she fed me. What she missed. Whether she should have caught it sooner.

My mom never gave up hope. We kept trying, but I gave up hope. She did not.

She was looking in the wrong place.

Not because she was careless.

Because nobody had handed her anywhere else to look.

You did not cause this.

I cannot tell you what is happening inside your son. No page can.

But I can tell you what my family finally found after eleven years, and it was not a parenting mistake. It was a mechanism.

What 19 doctors missed that his mom found on her own

The doctors had a word for me. Tourette's.

Sometimes anxiety, sometimes behavioral.

The labels rotated because none of them fit, and every ten-minute visit needed a word to write on the chart before the next prescription could follow.

The smoke kept changing shape, so they kept relabeling it.

My mother was the one who found the fire.

There is a condition where an infection gets in and the immune system never finishes the fight. Usually it is strep.

Ordinary strep throat, the kind that means a sore throat and a few days off school, the kind most kids clear without anyone thinking about it again.

Some of us cannot close it out.

The immune system keeps swinging at something that is already gone, and the swinging is what does the damage. It inflames the brain.

The basal ganglia, the part that runs motor control.

There is a name for it.

Pediatric Autoimmune Neuropsychiatric Disorders Associated with Streptococcal Infections.

Everyone just says PANDAS, and I will too.

And there is no test for it.

You cannot walk in and ask for the PANDAS test, because it does not exist.

It is blood work and symptom history and old infections and somebody patient enough to assemble the puzzle.

Most doctors do not have that kind of time for one kid. That is not a conspiracy. That is a waiting room.

Forty prescriptions were aimed at the smoke. The fire was in the immune system, and my mother was the only one who saw it.

And once you know something is burning, the order of operations changes.

You clear the infection first, and that is a doctor's job, usually antibiotics.

Then you go after the inflammation it left behind in the brain, because that is what is producing the symptoms you are watching.

The one treatment that works for inflammation (and why your child can't stay on it)

I want to be fair, because my mother deserved fairness on this.

Antibiotics to clear the infection are valid and necessary.

Anti-inflammatory steroids do help.

But nobody should be on steroids longer than about thirty days.

The side effects are tremendous and can be devastating.

So look at the corner this puts a mother in.

The inflammation needs to come down and stay down, for months.

The tool that does it best is the one her child has to stop taking after a month.

What she was really searching for at that kitchen table

That gap is what my mother was searching for at that kitchen table. She just did not have a word for it.

You already know the shape of it, even if nobody has ever said it back to you.

Not one more thing to add to the pile. You have a pile.

You have the magnesium and the diet change and the specialist eight months out.

You are the one who has to decide, every single time, whether the next thing is worth what it might cost him.

What you have been looking for is something that goes after what is actually driving this.

That he can stay on longer than a month.

And that does not hand you back a quieter kid who is somehow less himself.

Nobody told my mother that existed.

It took me until my twenties to find it, and I found it the worst possible way.
