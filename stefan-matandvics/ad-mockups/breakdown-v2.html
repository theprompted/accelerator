1

What These Ads Are

These are native image ads. They're designed to look and feel like content in the Facebook feed — not like advertising.

The goal is to stop the scroll and create curiosity before the viewer realizes they're looking at an ad. By then, they're already engaged.

Product Ads
  • Product on white background
  • Discount prominently featured
  • "Shop Now" energy
  • Reaches people ready to buy
Native Image Ads
  • Looks like content a friend posted
  • Stops scroll through recognition
  • "Wait, what?" energy
  • Creates demand in cold audiences

Both approaches have their place. Product ads work well for retargeting and people already shopping for socks. Native ads work for reaching people who don't know they have a sock problem yet — which is a much larger audience.

2

The 6 Image Concepts

Each image is designed to trigger a specific reaction — recognition, curiosity, or humor — that earns a few more seconds of attention.

The Evidence Hand holding a sock with a hole. "That's my drawer" recognition.
The Source Alpaca at a petting zoo. The animal itself is the punchline.
The Instructions Care label close-up. "Can you believe this?" energy.
The Drawer Overhead drawer shot. Authentic sock chaos.
The Morning Moment POV putting on socks. Everyday ritual.
The Comparison Two socks side by side. New vs. worn.

The images intentionally look like iPhone photos, not professional photography. This is strategic — polished advertising triggers "ad blindness" and gets scrolled past. Images that look like content get attention.

The Test

Would someone screenshot this image to share with a friend? If the answer is yes, it's native content. If no, it looks like an ad.

3

The Copy Pattern

Each ad follows the same structure:

1. Open with a contradiction. The first line states something that sounds wrong. This creates a "wait, what?" reaction that stops the scroll.

2. Acknowledge the tension. A short line like "That sounds backwards" validates the reaction and keeps them reading.

3. Reveal the hidden truth. Explain why the opening line is actually correct — not by attacking the reader, but by showing what the market has hidden from them.

Draft 1
"Cheap socks last longer than expensive ones."
Contradicts the assumption that price = quality
Draft 2
"Alpaca socks weren't designed for humans."
Contradicts the idea that alpaca is a premium luxury material
Draft 5
"If it needs cold water, it already failed."
Contradicts the idea that special care = high quality

The reader keeps their identity ("I make smart choices") while changing their understanding. They weren't wrong — they were working with incomplete information.

4

Where the Hooks Come From

The opening lines aren't invented in a brainstorming session. They're discovered in what's already true about the product.

"Your job is to be a sales detective. The hook exists in what makes the product remarkable. You just have to dig until you find it."

— John Carlton, direct response copywriter

Carlton's most famous ad was for a golf training program. After 2 hours of interviewing the client, he discovered that the instructor had one leg and could still outplay two-legged golfers. The client dismissed it as "old news." Carlton recognized it as gold. That ad ran for 8-10 years.

The contradiction was real — not manufactured. That's what made it work.

For Mat & Vic's, the contradictions already exist:

  • Cheap socks lasting longer than expensive ones (the blend engineering)
  • Alpaca fiber not being designed for humans (it's designed for alpacas in the Andes)
  • Washing machine care labels on "luxury" socks (admission of fragility)
  • "Premium" price not buying durability (just initial softness)

These are product truths. Stating them plainly creates cognitive dissonance — and curiosity.

5

The Full Journey

These ads are the first step. They create curiosity and drive traffic to the advertorial, which does the deeper work of shifting beliefs.

1
Ad stops the scroll
Image triggers recognition. Opening line creates a "wait, what?" reaction.
2
Copy builds curiosity
The reader keeps going because they need to understand why they're wrong.
3
Click to advertorial
"5 Reasons You Shouldn't Buy Premium Wool or Alpaca Socks"
4
Advertorial shifts beliefs
Deep education on why the premium sock market is broken.
5
Offer page closes
The reader now understands why Mat & Vic's is different.

By the time someone reaches the offer page, they're not comparing Mat & Vic's to other sock brands. They're comparing it to the broken system they just learned about.

The Shift

Product ads compete on price and features. This approach competes on understanding. Once someone understands why the market is broken, price becomes secondary.

6

What to Expect

Testing: These 6 drafts give us multiple angles to test. Some will outperform others — that's the point. We'll identify winners and iterate.

Metrics to watch:

  • CTR (Click-through rate) — Are people clicking to the advertorial?
  • CPC (Cost per click) — How efficiently are we driving traffic?
  • Time on page — Are people reading the advertorial or bouncing?
  • Conversion rate — Are advertorial readers becoming buyers?

Iteration: Based on early data, we'll double down on what's working and refine or retire what isn't. This is normal — the first round is about learning, not perfection.

Timeline: Real patterns usually emerge after a few hundred clicks. We'll review together once we have enough data to make informed decisions.
