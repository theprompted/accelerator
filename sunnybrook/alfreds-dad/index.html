Alfred's Experience With His Dad's Open Heart Surgery

Shared by Alfred with Andrew on February 5, 2026, ahead of Miranda's surgery on February 6, 2026.

Our experience

For us, it wasn't an easy journey, but the ending was very positive. Today, my dad is essentially fully recovered, with no lasting or new symptoms. The surgery itself was very successful, and we're incredibly grateful for the outcome.

That said, the road there was tough — and not at all linear.

On the day of surgery, there was a lot of waiting. From check-in to going into the OR was around 6–7 hours, and the surgery itself took roughly 4–5 hours. We stayed at the hospital the whole time.

The surgery was deemed successful. About 24 hours later, while he was in the CCU, my dad had a minor stroke. We were actually there when it happened — he suddenly started speaking gibberish, his blood pressure dropped, and nurses rushed in, asked us to step out, and took him for a CT scan.

They later told us it was a mini-stroke, which they said is not uncommon after open-heart surgery and often has no long-term effects — but at the time, it was very scary because it was a "wait and see" situation.

He stayed in the CCU for a couple of days and then was moved to the ward. At first, he seemed like he was improving, but then things gradually went downhill.

The heart itself was healing well throughout this entire time — the cardiac side of things was actually fine. The complication ended up being unrelated: his prostate. He had ongoing bleeding in his urine, which led to repeated catheter insertions (very painful), dropping hemoglobin levels, and blood pressure crashes.

Because of the blood loss, he would get dizzy, disoriented, and sometimes completely not himself. There were periods where he didn't recognize us, hallucinated, spoke nonsense, or just wasn't mentally "there." Looking back, all of that was due to blood loss — but at the time, we didn't know that.

He fell multiple times on the ward. Once, we got a call in the middle of the night saying he had been found passed out on the bathroom floor. Another time, it happened again. Each fall meant CT scans to check for head injury. There were also moments where he almost fell but we were there to catch him.

Because of this, we eventually had to intervene and say he needed constant supervision. His judgment wasn't there — when his blood pressure dropped, he would try to get up and go to the bathroom even when it clearly wasn't safe. At times, we basically had to treat him like a baby and override his decisions, which was emotionally very hard.

After the second fall, we spoke with the ward manager. The options were to hire someone overnight or get a private room. He was finally prioritized for a private room, and I stayed overnight with him for about a week to make sure he didn't try to get up and fall again.

It took about three weeks before they finally determined the prostate was the root cause of everything. Multiple specialists were involved — cardiology, neurology, urology — and rounds every day were critical. We tried to be there for rounds daily because shift changes meant people didn't always know the full story unless someone advocated for him.

Once the bleeding was controlled enough, he was discharged — much weaker than expected. He could only walk a few minutes with a walker and needed a lot of help. A couple of weeks later, he collapsed again at home, started bleeding again, and showed the same symptoms. We called an ambulance, went back to the ER, and this time they operated on his prostate. That finally fixed everything.

From that point on, recovery was much smoother. He did rehab, gradually regained strength, and today he says he's at 90–95%, but honestly, he looks completely normal to me.

Caregiver perspective (this part is mostly for you)

Looking back, everything makes sense. But while you're in it, you don't have answers. You don't know what's happening, what will fix it, or whether recovery will fully happen. That uncertainty is terrifying — and whatever you're feeling in the moment is completely valid. We felt all of it too.

Also, there were long stretches where my dad wasn't really aware of what was going on. He just wanted to go home, even when he wasn't ready. It wasn't a straight line at all — there were real regressions that made us question whether he'd fully recover.

Observations from the ward

Because we were on the ward for almost a month, we saw a lot of other patients come and go. My dad had four different roommates in the first few weeks — all of them were discharged in about a week or so after their surgery.

Most people we saw were discharged within 7–10 days, sometimes two weeks. A few rare cases stayed much longer (even months), usually with more severe or emergency cases. My dad was definitely one of the longer stays — largely because of the unrelated complication.

If that's any comfort: most people don't stay nearly as long.

Once someone is discharged and able to move more at home, recovery tends to accelerate. In the hospital, you're basically in bed all day except for short walks. At home, movement increases naturally.

If she's able, encourage walking — not just waiting for physio, but small walks with you, as long as it's safe. That made a big difference later on.

Practical things that helped

Hospitals are bright, loud, and uncomfortable. Anything you can do to make her feel more at home helps — music, a small speaker, familiar shows, personal items.

It's also very lonely there, especially if she's alert. Having someone around consistently helped my dad a lot — and helped us stay on top of communication between doctors and nurses as shifts changed.

I know you've got a lot on your plate — a small child, a baby on the way, and now this. Try to plan support so you're not carrying everything alone. And once she's discharged, having her stay with you (with help coming in) will likely help her recovery speed up — just being monitored, supported, and not alone.

Final thought

I truly hope her surgery and recovery are much smoother than what we experienced. But if things feel scary or unpredictable at times, know that it doesn't mean things won't turn out well.

Despite all the twists, my dad made a full recovery. The surgeons are very, very good at what they do — and often the hardest part isn't the surgery itself, but the recovery afterward.

Please also take care of yourself. Caregiving takes a huge amount of mental and emotional energy, especially once you're home and the nurses aren't there anymore.

I'm here if you want to talk through anything as it comes up.

— Alfred
