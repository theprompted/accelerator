Ad creative examples from Deep Danieling
Response

Built 10 native image ad concepts for PE. But before you look at the images, I want to walk you through how we got here — because the process matters more than the deliverables. You can use this same process for any product, any niche.

Step 1 — Who's actually buying?

We dug into your age breakdown data and found something counterintuitive. The best unit economics aren't coming from the 55–65 men your LP was built for. They're coming from men aged 40–49.

Why? Not because PE is more common at that age — the prevalence is roughly equal across brackets. It's because PE hitting a man in his 40s often feels new. It's acquired, not lifelong. He didn't have this problem before. And when something disappears that used to work, you move faster to fix it than someone who's been living with it for decades.

The insight

A 42-year-old man who's had PE for 2 years is more motivated than a 62-year-old who's had it for 20. The 42-year-old is frustrated and refusing to accept it. The 62-year-old has half-resigned. Frustration converts. Resignation doesn't.

So we built our ad avatar around the 40–49 man specifically. Peak career. Kids under 12. Wife is frustrated, not resigned — she still cares, which makes the tension worse. He's elder Millennial or young Gen X. He doesn't see himself as old. He doesn't relate to ads showing grey-haired couples. He sees himself as the guy who still has it together — except for this one thing.

Step 2 — First round failed

I generated the first batch of AI images. Out of 10, I'd keep 1. The rest were technically correct but generic. Blue-toned bedrooms. Exercise balls. Candles on a nightstand. Clean, tasteful, medical-looking.

The problem: a man scrolling Facebook would not look at these images and instantly know they're about PE. They looked like ads for mattresses, or supplements, or relationship coaching. They required the headline to do all the work.

The sight-read test

If a man with PE can't look at the image alone — no headline, no copy — and feel a flash of recognition, the image is wrong. The image has to do the targeting, not the words. Words come second.

Step 3 — Research, not guessing

Instead of generating more images and hoping, I went to the places where men actually talk about PE. Reddit forums — r/PrematureEjaculation, r/sex, r/sexover30. Clinical studies on treatment-seeking behavior. I wasn't looking for what PE is. I was looking for the objects and scenes men describe when they talk about living with it.

What kept coming up, over and over:

The failed solutions. Delay sprays sitting on the nightstand. Numbing creams in the medicine cabinet. Bottles of Sertraline prescribed off-label. Thick condom boxes bought specifically for the desensitizing effect. Supplement bottles promising "stamina" and "endurance." Benzocaine wipes. EMLA tubes. A drawer full of things that didn't work.

The breakthrough

The failed solutions are the symptomatic imagery. Every man who's spent a night at 1am researching PE has encountered these products. They don't need a headline to know what the ad is about. The product itself is the signal.

Step 4 — Dog whistle copy

Once I knew the images would show PE products (delay sprays, pill bottles, numbing wipes, supplement graveyards), I realized the label text on those products could do double duty.

Words like Lidocaine, Sertraline, Benzocaine, Dapoxetine — these serve two purposes at once:

Purpose 1 — Algorithmic targeting. Facebook's image recognition reads text on products. When it sees pharmaceutical and supplement terminology, it places the ad into health/wellness interest clusters. The algo does your targeting for you.

Purpose 2 — User recognition. A man who's been researching PE at 1am has seen these words dozens of times. Lidocaine on a delay spray label. Sertraline in an article about off-label SSRI use for PE. Benzocaine on a numbing wipe packet. These words ring a bell that only rings for the right man. Everyone else scrolls past.

Dog whistle copy

The label text isn't "copy" in the traditional sense. It's a frequency that only the target audience can hear. The man with PE sees it and freezes. The man without PE sees a random product photo and keeps scrolling. That's the filter you want.

Step 5 — Three rounds of refinement

Even with the right concept, the first AI-generated images had wrong labels — the model would hallucinate product names that don't exist, or swap in unrelated pharmaceutical terms. "Saline Nasal Spray" on a delay spray. "Miconazole Nitrate" on a PE supplement.

We solved this by structurally separating the scene description from the label text in the AI prompts — telling the model exactly what words to put on every label, isolated from the rest of the image description. That fixed it. Labels came out correct on the first try after that.

Three rounds total. First round: wrong concept (generic imagery). Second round: right concept, wrong labels. Third round: right concept, right labels, paired with ad copy.

04
"Product arrives in a plain box. Should I create an instruction/usage plan insert?"
Response

Yes. Keep it simple — one page, first 30 days, clear protocol. It's a compliance tool. Most people use a device like this inconsistently, and inconsistency kills results. A plan in the box sets expectations, drives follow-through, and reduces refunds.

05
"When does bulk ordering, Shopify Plus, white labelling become relevant?"
Response

The impulse to move fast here is right — but the sequence matters.

Bulk ordering, Shopify Plus, white labelling — these all cost real money upfront at a time when you're still proving out the trajectory. An ad account can go down. A creative can stop working. Volume that felt permanent last week can look very different next week. Committing cash to a bulk order or a higher Shopify plan during that window adds stress you don't need when you should be focused on the marketing.

What I'd want to see first: two consecutive weeks at 100+ orders/day. Not one week, two. That's the confidence signal. Consistency is what separates a momentum window from a real baseline.

In the meantime — don't wait on the prep work. Start having conversations with your supplier now. Negotiate bulk pricing before you need it, so when you're ready to pull the trigger the deal is already structured. Same with packaging design and insert artwork — get that moving in the background so it's ready to go the moment you hit your number.

On payment terms: if you can negotiate paying the invoice on shipment rather than upfront, do that. It takes time to get there — suppliers need to build trust with you first — but it's worth pursuing early.

The rule: work toward it now, trigger it in 10–14 days if volume holds. Aggressive ordering early has a way of biting back. You'll be glad you waited.

"PE gets a lot of spend but not enough sales today — raise budget or leave it?"
Response

Leave it. One bad day isn't a signal. Attribution is mostly art, not science — what's happening in the background may not show up until tomorrow or the day after.

Separate deliberate move: tighten cost controls 10% across the board — not as a reaction to this day, but as a strategic efficiency nudge. Watch over 3–5 days, not one.

07
"My goal is to reach an overall ROAS of 2 and be steady around it."
Response

Right direction. Technically, 20% contribution margin (VAT-inclusive) requires ~2.16x gross ROAS at your scale. The 10% tighten gets you part of the way there. Watch 7–10 days. If volume holds, tighten another 10%. Slow and intentional over a big swing.
