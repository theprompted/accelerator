Response

Built 10 native image ad concepts for PE. Before you look at them, here's the thinking behind what's in them and why — because the approach is the real deliverable.

Starting point: who are we talking to?

We targeted men aged 40–49 for four reasons. First, PE prevalence is flat across age groups — but men in their 40s experience it as new. Acquired, not lifelong. When something disappears that used to work, you move faster to fix it. Loss aversion is one of the strongest purchase drivers there is. Second, peer-reviewed research on DTC men's health shows men 40–59 are the most likely to buy online — old enough to have a real problem, young enough to be digitally comfortable, embarrassed enough to prefer a purchase over a doctor visit. Third, men 45–54 already spend over $6,000/year on healthcare. ₪399 fits into existing spending without resistance. Fourth, your own ad data confirms it — the PE angle is outperforming everything else.

That combination is why the creative is built specifically for the 40–49 man. Peak career, kids under 12, elder Millennial or young Gen X. He doesn't see himself as old. He doesn't relate to grey-haired couples in ads. And he's not resigned — he's frustrated and looking for something that actually works.

What the images show — and what they don't

The core decision: every image shows the PE problem world, not the solution world. Delay sprays on a nightstand. Numbing creams in a medicine cabinet. Supplement bottles promising "stamina." Benzocaine wipes. A drawer full of things that didn't work.

Why these objects specifically? Because we went to the places where men actually talk about PE — forums, communities, clinical literature — and looked for the objects and scenes they describe when they talk about living with it. The failed solutions kept coming up, over and over. Every man who's spent a night at 1am researching PE has encountered these products.

The sight-read test

If a man with PE can't look at the image alone — no headline, no copy — and feel a flash of recognition, the image is wrong. The image has to do the targeting, not the words.

What we deliberately avoided: anything abstract or "tasteful." Blue-toned bedrooms, exercise balls, candles, couples sitting apart. These could be ads for mattresses, supplements, relationship coaching — anything. They require the headline to do all the work. The images need to be unmistakable on their own.

The label text is doing more than you think

Words like Lidocaine, Sertraline, Benzocaine, Dapoxetine on the product labels serve two purposes at once:

Algorithmic targeting. Facebook's image recognition reads text on products. Pharmaceutical and supplement terminology places the ad into health/wellness interest clusters. The algo does your targeting work for you.

User recognition. A man who's been researching PE has seen these words dozens of times. Lidocaine on a delay spray. Sertraline in an article about off-label SSRI use for PE. Benzocaine on a numbing wipe packet. These words ring a bell that only rings for the right man. Everyone else scrolls past.

Dog whistle copy

The label text isn't copy in the traditional sense. It's a frequency that only the target audience can hear. The man with PE sees it and freezes. The man without PE sees a random product photo and keeps scrolling. That's the filter you want.

Each image is paired with ad copy that completes the story the image starts.

Response

The offer is fine — 50% off second unit is strong. 12% isn't bad, but there's room to push it higher. Two phases to get there — one is copy, the other is offer structure.

Phase 1 — Copy Changes (test first)

The current popup uses a generic discount frame ("special offer"). Men buying a PE device aren't thinking about a backup for travel. They're thinking about who they're becoming. Rewrote the popup around a transformation arc. New headline: "Here's How You Can Go From Lasting 60 Seconds To 60 Minutes." Body copy built around the archetype — the man who doesn't leave anything to chance. Decline link with identity cost: "No thanks, 60 seconds is fine for now." Same offer, better framing. This alone should move the take rate. You can run multiple rounds here — test different headlines, different decline links, different transformation arcs — all without touching the offer or pricing. Each round gives you a new baseline to beat.

Phase 2 — Offer Changes (after Phase 1 baseline)

Once you've squeezed what you can from copy, layer in offer changes: bundle the second ViroCore with the From 60 Seconds to 60 Minutes Transformation Program — a 60-day daily email program. ₪400 value, free with the second device. Starts the moment he orders — no waiting for the device. Days 1–20: understand what's been happening in your body. Days 21–40: use ViroCore alongside daily protocols. Days 41–60: go the distance, consistently, every time. This adds perceived value without changing your physical product pricing. And there are multiple offer changes you can test here too — different bonuses, different program lengths, different value anchors — all without adjusting the price of the device itself.

"When does bulk ordering, Shopify Plus, white labelling become relevant?"
Response

The impulse to move fast here is right — but the sequence matters.

Bulk ordering, Shopify Plus, white labelling — these all cost real money upfront at a time when you're still proving out the trajectory. An ad account can go down. A creative can stop working. Volume that felt permanent last week can look very different next week. Committing cash to a bulk order or a higher Shopify plan during that window adds stress you don't need when you should be focused on the marketing.

What I'd want to see first: two consecutive weeks at 50+ orders/day. Not one week, two. That's the confidence signal. Consistency is what separates a momentum window from a real baseline.

In the meantime — don't wait on the prep work. Start having conversations with your supplier now. Negotiate bulk pricing before you need it, so when you're ready to pull the trigger the deal is already structured. Same with packaging design and insert artwork — get that moving in the background so it's ready to go the moment you hit your number.

On payment terms: if you can negotiate paying the invoice on shipment rather than upfront, do that. It takes time to get there — suppliers need to build trust with you first — but it's worth pursuing early.

De-risking: compliance before scale

This is also a good time to start thinking about cleaning up claims. Some of the statements on the current LP are exaggerated — they work at low volume but become a liability at scale. Ad accounts get flagged. Payment processors pull funding. Competitors report you. The move is to gradually replace embellished claims with things that are actually true, but strategically worded for persuasion. You can be just as compelling without fabricating. The goal is marketing that holds up under scrutiny.

The rule: work toward it now, trigger it in 10–14 days if volume holds. Aggressive ordering early has a way of biting back. You'll be glad you waited.

Separate deliberate move: tighten cost controls 10% across the board — not as a reaction to this day, but as a strategic efficiency nudge. Watch over 3–5 days, not one.

"For PE — test multiple LPs for different personas, or test lots of ads to the current LP?"
Response

Test ads, not LPs — for now. Your LP is working. There's still a lot of juice left in it.

The nuance is which ads. We did a deep dive into your age breakdown data and the man who's buying most efficiently is 40–49. Not because PE is more common at that age — it's actually about the same across all brackets. It's that PE hitting a man in his 40s often feels new. It wasn't there before. And when something disappears that you used to have, you move faster to get it back.

This doesn't mean changing your targeting in Meta. Let the CBO do its thing. What it means is when you're building the next round of creatives, you're thinking about the 40–49 man when you write the hook, pick the image, choose the angle. His version of the problem looks different than the 60-year-old's. Different relationship dynamic. Different identity arc. Different fear.

Your current LP was built with the 55–65 man in mind — the testimonials skew that way. It's converting. Don't touch it. But the best economics in your data are coming from men 10–15 years younger than who the funnel was written for. That's the gap. More ads aimed at that man, landing on the same page — that's where the next wave comes from.
